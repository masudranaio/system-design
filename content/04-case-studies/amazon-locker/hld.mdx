This lesson zooms out from a single locker station to the network that
operates thousands of them. It builds directly on the object model from
the [Amazon Locker LLD](/case-studies/amazon-locker/lld) — the same
`Compartment`, `Package`, and `AccessToken` concepts still apply inside
one station — but everything about *finding* a station, *coordinating*
reservations across it, and *staying available* when a station drops
offline is a different problem, and that's what this lesson covers.

## Problem framing

The LLD asks: given one station with 30-150 compartments, how do you
deposit and retrieve a package correctly? The HLD asks a different
question: given a few thousand of those stations spread across a
country, how does a customer in Austin find a station near their office
with an open medium compartment, reserve it before anyone else does, and
still get service if that exact station's cellular modem drops for an
hour on a Saturday?

That reframes the problem three ways:

- **Geo-discovery.** "Find a locker near me with capacity" is a spatial
  query over thousands of moving-target availability counts, not a
  lookup on one station's compartment list.
- **Coordinated reservation.** Two customers browsing the same
  neighborhood can try to claim the same compartment within milliseconds
  of each other, and the system spans many app servers with no single
  in-memory lock to serialize them against.
- **Fleet-wide connectivity.** Every station is a physical device on a
  cellular or Wi-Fi uplink, not a service behind a stable data-center
  network — some fraction of the fleet is always mid-reconnect, and the
  design has to keep working through that instead of treating it as an
  outage.

None of this is "the LLD problem at bigger numbers." It's a distributed
IoT and reservation-coordination problem where the single-station logic
from the LLD is just one component running inside each station.

<QuizItem
  question="Why isn't the HLD just 'the LLD problem, but with more stations'?"
  answer="The LLD's hard problem is single-station concurrency (two couriers, one compartment). The HLD's hard problems are different in kind: spatial search across stations that aren't co-located, reservation coordination across many stateless app servers instead of one in-memory station, and tolerating individual stations going offline — none of which exist at single-station scale."

## Requirements & capacity estimate

**Functional requirements:**

- Locker discovery by geo-radius plus a package-size filter
- Atomic slot reservation that prevents two concurrent requests from
  double-booking the same compartment
- Deposit and pickup at any of the network's stations
- Device health and telemetry monitoring per station
- Over-the-air (OTA) firmware updates pushed to the fleet
- Returns coordination, including scheduling carrier pickup of
  return-pending packages

**Non-functional requirements:**

- Peak load of roughly **500,000 QPS** on the capacity-reservation path
- Availability queries answered in **under 100ms**
- Physical unlock latency of **2-3 seconds** from code entry
- **>95% cache hit rate** on the availability-query path
- **24/7** operation with graceful degradation when a station is offline
- Compartment mix of roughly 40% small / 35% medium / 20% large / 5%
  oversized, with 30-150 compartments per station
- Prime-Day-style events causing **10-20x** volume spikes in affected
  metros

**Back-of-envelope math for the 500,000 QPS peak.** Start from the
network's baseline write volume — actual completed deposits and pickups
— at roughly 2,000 events/sec across the whole network on an ordinary
day. Every completed event is preceded by many availability reads:
a customer pans a map, filters by size, and often re-checks after a
first choice turns out full by the time they tap "reserve." Call that
read amplification ~15x, which puts baseline read traffic on the
availability path around 30,000 QPS. Layer a Prime-Day-style spike of
~17x (inside the stated 10-20x range) on top of that baseline, and the
peak lands at roughly 30,000 × 17 ≈ **500,000 QPS** — which is why the
read path, not the write path, is what has to survive the event (more
on that in the worked example).

**Back-of-envelope math for network capacity.** Assume the network
operates on the order of 5,000 stations (consistent with "thousands").
At an average of 90 compartments/station (midpoint of the 30-150 range),
that's **450,000 compartments** network-wide — roughly 180,000 small,
157,500 medium, 90,000 large, and 22,500 oversized, following the stated
size mix. That total is the ceiling the caching and reservation design
below has to serve queries against without hitting the database on
every read.

_The gateway fans requests out to five focused services instead of one
monolith; the reservation path gets its own service and cache stack
because it alone has to sustain 500,000 QPS, while station telemetry
flows over a separate async channel so a spike in device chatter can't
compete with customer-facing reads._

Two design choices are doing the heavy lifting here:

- **Capacity Reservation Service is split out from Locker Management
  Service**, even though both ultimately touch compartment state.
  Reservation is the narrow, latency-critical hot path (sub-100ms reads,
  500,000 QPS peak); Locker Management handles lower-frequency
  station-health and configuration operations. Splitting them means a
  burst of device telemetry or a firmware rollout can't compete for the
  same connection pool as a customer trying to reserve a compartment.
- **Two data stores, not one.** Postgres holds transactional state
  (compartments, packages, reservations) where correctness matters more
  than write throughput. A time-series store holds telemetry (heartbeat,
  door-sensor events, battery voltage) where the write volume is high,
  each point is small, and queries are almost always "show me the last N
  minutes for station X" — a shape relational tables handle poorly at
  IoT-fleet scale.

### Geo-discovery and reservation under contention

"Find lockers within 2 miles with a medium slot open" is a spatial range
query, and running it as a full scan over 450,000 compartments per
request is what makes 100ms latency impossible at 500,000 QPS. The fix
is a **geohash** — an encoding that turns a latitude/longitude pair into
a short string (e.g. `9q8yy`) such that nearby points share a string
prefix. "Find everything within 2 miles" becomes "find everything whose
geohash starts with this prefix," which is an indexed prefix lookup
instead of a geometric scan over every station's coordinates.

{/* concept-dependency: HLD-11 not yet built, explained inline */}

Once a customer picks a station, the reservation itself has to survive
concurrent attempts. The design uses **optimistic concurrency control
(OCC)**: every compartment row carries a `version` number; a reservation
reads the current version, then issues `UPDATE compartments SET status
= 'reserved', version = version + 1 WHERE id = ? AND version = ?`. If
another request already reserved that compartment, the version no
longer matches, the update affects zero rows, and the service returns a
conflict — the customer is shown the next-nearest match instead. This is
deliberately chosen over a **pessimistic lock** (acquire a lock on the
row, block other requests, release after the transaction): pessimistic
locking pays a blocking cost on *every* request in exchange for
correctness that's only actually needed on the rare request that
collides. Given that any single compartment is contended by two
customers only occasionally, OCC pays the cost — a retry — only when a
collision actually happens.

{/* concept-dependency: HLD-10 not yet built, explained inline */}

At extreme throughput, even OCC needs help: if every reservation attempt
across the whole network competes for connections against every other
attempt, the database connection pool becomes the bottleneck regardless
of how cheap each individual update is. The design pre-allocates
**regional reservation queues** — requests are bucketed by geographic
region (roughly metro-area granularity) into separate worker pools, so
contention is bounded to reservations that could plausibly collide (same
station, same region) rather than serialized against unrelated
reservations on the other side of the country.

<QuizItem
  question="Two customers in the same city both try to reserve the last available medium compartment at the same station within a 50ms window. Under optimistic concurrency control, what happens?"
  answer="Both read the compartment row at version v. Whichever conditional UPDATE ... WHERE version = v commits first wins, and the row moves to v+1. The second UPDATE matches zero rows because the version already changed, so that request gets a conflict response and the losing customer is offered the next-nearest available compartment — no lock was held while either request was in flight."

### Multi-level caching for the read path

At 500,000 QPS peak with a sub-100ms target, hitting Postgres on every
availability read is not an option — even a well-indexed geo-sharded
query at that volume saturates connection pools long before it saturates
disk I/O. The design layers four tiers between the customer and the
source of truth:

<DiagramPanel
  type="architecture"
  title="Availability query — cache read path"
  chart={`graph LR
  Req[Availability query] --> L1{L1 in-process cache<br/>TTL 5-10s}
  L1 -- hit, less than 1ms --> Resp[Response]
  L1 -- miss --> L2{L2 Redis<br/>write-through}
  L2 -- hit, 1-5ms --> Resp
  L2 -- miss --> L3{L3 regional snapshot<br/>refreshed every 5-15s}
  L3 -- hit --> Resp
  L3 -- miss or stale --> L4[(L4 geo-sharded Postgres<br/>source of truth)]
  L4 --> Backfill[Backfill L1, L2, L3]
  Backfill --> Resp
`}
/>

_Each tier absorbs the volume the one behind it can't sustain — L1 is
per-instance and free of network hops, L2 is shared across instances so
a miss on one server can hit on another, and L3 exists because even a
fast key-value hit still costs a scatter-gather across many keys to
build a ranked "nearest N compartments" answer; L3 precomputes that
answer directly._

{/* concept-dependency: HLD-06 not yet built, explained inline */}
{/* concept-dependency: HLD-07 not yet built, explained inline */}

L2 uses **write-through** caching: when a reservation changes a
compartment's status, the write goes to Redis and Postgres together
(rather than **cache-aside**, where the cache is only populated lazily
on the next read miss), because a reservation-critical path can't afford
to serve a stale "available" answer to the very next customer who reads
it. L4, Postgres, is **geo-sharded** — partitioned by region so a query
for Chicago-area lockers only ever touches the Chicago shard's data and
its read replicas, never scanning rows for stations 2,000 miles away.
That >95% cache hit rate target means fewer than 1 in 20 requests ever
reaches L4 at all; the shard layout is there for the requests that do,
and for the L3 snapshot jobs that periodically rebuild from it.

<QuizItem
  question="Why does the L3 regional snapshot exist at all if L2 Redis already caches the same underlying data?"
  answer="A single Redis GET is fast, but answering 'nearest N available medium compartments near this point' from raw per-compartment keys still requires a scatter-gather across many keys per request. L3 precomputes and stores that ranked answer directly, refreshed every 5-15 seconds, so the hot path becomes a single lookup instead of an aggregation done on every request."
/>

### IoT fleet and offline resilience

Every station is a physical device on a cellular or Wi-Fi uplink, and at
fleet scale some stations are always mid-reconnect. Two channels connect
a station to the rest of the system:

- An **asynchronous telemetry channel over MQTT** — a lightweight
  publish/subscribe protocol built for devices with intermittent
  connectivity and limited bandwidth. Stations publish heartbeat, door-sensor
  events, power status, and firmware version; the Locker Management
  Service subscribes and writes them into the time-series store.
- A **synchronous unlock-command path**, authenticated per-device with
  **TLS/X.509** — a certificate-based identity scheme where each station
  holds a unique certificate the central system verifies before trusting
  a command or accepting a report, so a compromised or spoofed device
  can't unlock compartments it doesn't own.

{/* concept-dependency: HLD-10 not yet built, explained inline */}

Neither channel assumes the station is always reachable. Each station
keeps a **local cache of active pickup codes** synced ahead of time, so
a customer entering a valid code can pick up a package even if the
station's uplink is down at that exact moment — the station validates
the code against its own cache rather than calling the central service
synchronously for every pickup. Deposit and pickup events that happen
offline go into a **local retry queue with exponential backoff**: the
station keeps attempting to flush the queue to the central system,
waiting longer between attempts each time it fails, until connectivity
returns and the events **reconcile** — the central system replays them
against the source-of-truth database and resolves anything that
conflicted with events it saw from elsewhere in the meantime. A
**battery backup rated for 30-60 minutes** keeps the station's local
compute (not the compartment locks themselves) running through short
power blips so the local cache and queue survive them.

Lock hardware itself makes a physical trade-off: **fail-secure** locks
stay locked on power loss, protecting whatever is already stored but
stranding a courier's undelivered package and anyone mid-pickup;
**fail-open** locks release on power loss, guaranteeing access but
risking theft during the outage window. Neither is uniformly correct —
a network built for 24/7 self-service leans fail-secure by default (a
stranded package is recoverable; a stolen one isn't), but stations
storing higher-value contents may configure specific compartments
fail-open under supervised conditions instead.

<QuizItem
  question="A station's cellular connection drops for 40 minutes during a busy Saturday. Why does pickup still work during that window?"
  answer="The station keeps a local cache of active pickup codes synced before it went offline, so it can validate a code and physically unlock the compartment without calling the central service. The resulting deposit/pickup event is queued locally with exponential-backoff retry and reconciled against the source of truth once connectivity returns."

## Trade-offs

**Consistency vs. availability for locker state.** The design chooses
**eventual consistency**: a station keeps accepting deposits and
pickups while offline and reconciles afterward, over **strong
consistency**, where a deposit blocks until the central system confirms
it. Strong consistency would guarantee no station ever diverges from the
source of truth, but it means a station with a flaky uplink stops
accepting packages entirely during every blip — for a fleet where some
fraction of stations are always mid-reconnect, that's a worse failure
mode than the rare double-booking eventual consistency has to catch and
resolve on reconciliation.

{/* concept-dependency: HLD-01 not yet built, explained inline */}

This is the same tension the CAP theorem names for any distributed
system: when a network partition happens (here, a station losing its
uplink), you can't have both full consistency and full availability
across the partition, so the design has to pick. Amazon Locker picks
availability at the station and accepts short-lived inconsistency,
because a locker a courier can't currently reach is worse than a locker
whose state briefly disagrees with the network.

**Local vs. centralized code verification.** Following from the same
choice, pickup codes are validated **locally** against the station's
synced cache rather than **centrally** against the live database on
every attempt. Local verification is what makes offline pickup possible
at all, but it means a code revoked centrally (say, a customer reports
theft) doesn't take effect at that station until its next sync — a
window centralized verification wouldn't have, at the cost of pickup
failing outright whenever the station can't reach the network.

**Reservation aggressiveness.** A **conservative hold** — a reservation
that's guaranteed to stay valid until the customer uses or abandons it —
is simple and never double-books, but it ties up a compartment for the
full hold window even for customers who never show up, lowering network
utilization. An **aggressive, probabilistic release** — freeing a
reservation early based on a predicted low chance the customer follows
through, informed by historical dwell-time patterns — raises utilization
but introduces a real (if small) chance of releasing a compartment out
from under a customer who does show up late. The base design uses
conservative holds; probabilistic release is a genuine extension for a
network under sustained capacity pressure, not the default.

**Precomputed regional snapshots vs. on-demand computation.** Serving
"nearest N available compartments" from a precomputed L3 snapshot means
answers can be up to 5-15 seconds stale. Computing that ranked answer
fresh on every request would eliminate staleness, but doing that
scatter-gather-and-rank computation at 500,000 QPS is the same cost
problem the caching tiers exist to avoid in the first place — on-demand
computation isn't a real alternative at this volume, only a slower path
that still ends up needing heavy caching in front of it.

  question="Why does the Capacity Reservation Service choose eventual consistency for station state instead of blocking every deposit on central confirmation?"
  answer="Blocking on central confirmation means a station that's briefly offline can't accept any deposits at all. Eventual consistency trades a rare double-booking — caught and resolved on reconciliation — for keeping every station able to accept packages through short connectivity blips, which is the much more common case at fleet scale."

## Worked example

Trace one customer's flow end to end: discovery, reservation under the
caching tiers, a courier's deposit through a brief offline window, and
reconciliation back to the source of truth.

  Customer->>App: selects Station #4471, reserves medium slot
  App->>CRS: reserve(compartmentId, version=v)
  CRS->>PG: UPDATE compartments SET status='reserved', version=v+1 WHERE version=v
  PG-->>CRS: 1 row updated, version now v+1
  CRS-->>App: reservation confirmed, access code issued
  CRS->>Station: sync reservation + code to local cache
  Note over Station: cellular connectivity drops
  Note over Station: courier arrives, enters code
  Station->>Station: validate code against local cache (offline)
  Station->>Station: unlock, mark compartment occupied locally
  Station->>Station: queue deposit-confirmed event, retry with backoff
  Note over Station: connectivity restored
  Station->>CRS: flush queued deposit-confirmed event
  CRS->>PG: reconcile: confirm occupancy in source of truth
  CRS->>Redis: invalidate stale entry
  CRS->>L3: invalidate stale entry
  CRS->>Notif: trigger pickup-code notification
  Notif-->>Customer: pickup code, 3-day window
`}
/>

_Nothing here is hypothetical padding — every step maps to a piece of
the design above: the geohash-prefix search, the four-tier cache falling
through to Postgres on a cold path, the versioned conditional update
that would reject a losing concurrent reservation, and the
offline-then-reconcile flow that keeps the deposit valid even though the
station couldn't reach the network at the moment it happened._

Two details are easy to miss on a first read. First, the reservation
sync to the station happens **before** the courier arrives, not at
deposit time — that's what makes the offline deposit possible at all;
if the station only learned about the reservation when the courier
showed up, an offline station couldn't validate a delivery it had never
heard of. Second, the cache invalidation happens explicitly, as its own
step, rather than waiting for L2's TTL or L3's next refresh cycle — a
compartment that's now physically occupied can't be left showing
"available" for even the 5-15 seconds until the next natural refresh,
because that's exactly the window a second customer could reserve a slot
that's already full.

## Interview angle

**"How do you prevent two customers from reserving the same compartment
at the same time across two different app servers?"** Point at the
versioned conditional update: no server holds an in-memory lock, so
"two different app servers" isn't actually a special case — every
reservation attempt, from any server, races against the same database
row's version number, and the database itself is what arbitrates the
winner.

**"A locker station loses internet for an hour during a busy period —
what happens to in-flight pickups and deposits?"** Walk through the
offline-resilience deep dive: local code cache for pickups, local retry
queue with backoff for deposits, reconciliation once connectivity
returns. The interviewer is checking whether you reach for "the station
just fails" as the default answer, or whether you've designed the
offline path as a first-class case instead of an afterthought.

**"Prime Day causes a 15x spike in one metro area — where does the
system bend or break first, and how do you protect it?"** The read path
bends first, not the write path — the back-of-envelope math shows read
volume amplified well beyond actual deposit/pickup volume, so cache
eviction pressure and L3 staleness show up before reservation throughput
does. The answer isn't "add more app servers": it's regional isolation
(the affected metro's shard and cache tier absorb the spike without
starving other regions) plus, if sustained, temporarily widening L3's
refresh interval to trade a little more staleness for headroom.

### Open design challenge

Extend the network-scale design to support **dynamic, demand-aware
locker placement**: given historical utilization and dwell-time data per
station, design a subsystem that recommends where to add new locker
capacity (or reallocate compartment-size mix) in a city. What data would
you pipe from the Package and Reservation services into this subsystem,
how would you avoid it becoming a bottleneck on the hot reservation
path, and how does this connect to dwell-time prediction?

<details>
<summary>Reference answer</summary>

Add a **Placement Recommendation Service** that never sits on the
reservation hot path — it consumes an asynchronous event stream (a
Kafka-style topic) populated by state-change events the Package and
Reservation services already emit: `reservation-created`,
`deposit-confirmed`, `pickup-confirmed`, and `expired`/`returned`. Each
event carries station ID, compartment size, timestamps for reservation
and pickup, and outcome. The reservation hot path publishes to the
stream fire-and-forget; it never blocks on, or calls into, the
Placement Recommendation Service.

An hourly or nightly batch job aggregates that stream (or a warehouse
table fed by change-data-capture from Postgres, not the primary
transactional database itself) into two things per station and
compartment size: **utilization** (fraction of compartments of that size
occupied, sampled hourly) and **dwell-time percentiles** (p50/p90 time
from deposit to pickup). A separate dwell-time prediction model —
a regression over package size, day of week, and the station's
historical dwell distribution — feeds forward-looking estimates into the
same aggregation, so the recommendation isn't just reacting to what
already happened but anticipating demand for the size mix a station will
need next month.

The scoring step compares observed-plus-predicted demand against
installed capacity per size class and produces concrete, station-level
output — e.g. "Station #4471: reallocate 10 small compartments to
medium; trailing-30-day small-slot utilization is 22% vs. medium-slot
utilization 96%" or "add ~40 compartments within a 1-mile radius of
(lat, lng), based on a sustained rejected-reservation rate in that
area." The isolation from the hot path comes from two things: the
subsystem only ever reads from the async stream or a downstream
warehouse copy, never the primary geo-sharded transactional database,
and its batch jobs run on their own schedule rather than in response to
individual reservation events.

</details>
