title: "Ride-Sharing: High-Level Design"
---

Uber's hard problem isn't drawing a car icon moving on a map — it's that
both sides of the match are moving continuously, in both directions, all
the time. A rider requesting a trip needs a driver assigned in low
single-digit seconds, out of a pool of drivers who are themselves driving
around, going on and off duty, and finishing other trips, second by
second, across an entire city.

## Problem framing

Two coupled hard problems, not one:

- **Find the nearest available driver**, fast, among millions of moving
  points citywide — this is a **geospatial-indexing problem first**, not a
  generic matching/queueing problem. "Scan every driver row and compute
  distance to the rider" is an O(n) table scan against a table that's
  being rewritten every few seconds by every driver in the city — it
  doesn't survive contact with a dense downtown at rush hour.
- **Price the trip fairly** when supply and demand can swing sharply
  minute to minute — a concert letting out, a rainstorm starting, a
  stadium emptying all pull demand into a small area faster than drivers
  can physically reposition into it.

<Point icon="clock">
  A rider opening the app expects an assigned driver in ~2-5 seconds. That
  budget has to cover: finding candidate drivers, ranking them, sending an
  offer, and getting an acceptance — not just the search step alone.
</Point>
<Point icon="branch">
  Unlike Ticketmaster's checkout, which is a single hot resource contended
  by many buyers, ride matching is many-to-many: many riders and many
  drivers, both moving, matched continuously across a whole city at once.
</Point>

{/* concept-dependency: HLD-02 not yet built, explained inline */}
The reason this is a geospatial-indexing problem rather than a plain
matching problem: matching algorithms (bipartite matching, greedy
assignment) assume you already have a short list of candidates to choose
between. Getting from "every driver in the city" down to "the ~20 drivers
actually near this rider" is the part a naive design gets wrong, and it's
solved with a spatial index, not a smarter matching algorithm.

<QuizItem
  question="Why is 'geospatial indexing' called out as a separate problem from 'matching,' rather than folding both into one 'find and assign a driver' step?"
  answer="Because they solve different things: geospatial indexing narrows millions of moving drivers down to a small nearby candidate set fast; matching then decides which candidate to assign. A fast matching algorithm run against an unindexed full scan of every driver in the city is still slow, because the bottleneck is finding candidates, not choosing among them."
/>

## Requirements & capacity estimate

**Functional requirements:**

- Rider requests a ride: pickup location, destination, ride type
  (economy / pool / premium)
- System matches the rider to a nearby available driver
- Driver can accept or reject (bounded timeout) a match offer
- Rider and driver see live location tracking during the trip
- System computes ETA — to pickup, and to destination
- System computes trip price, including surge/dynamic pricing
- Trip moves through a defined lifecycle: requested → matched → en route
  to pickup → in progress → completed / cancelled
- Rider and driver rate each other after trip completion

**Non-functional requirements:**

- Matching latency: assigned driver in ~2-5s, even in dense cities
- Location freshness: driver position updates reach nearby searches
  within ~2-4s — drivers ping every few seconds, and briefly stale
  location is an acceptable trade-off for availability
- Scale: ~1M+ concurrent active trips globally at peak, tens of millions
  of location pings/sec system-wide, matching decisions made per
  city/region independently
- Availability over strict consistency for driver *location* — an
  occasional match against a driver whose position moved slightly is
  recoverable
- Strong consistency for "one driver, one active trip at a time" — no
  double-dispatch of the same driver

**Out of scope for this lesson:** payment processing internals, driver
background-check/onboarding, turn-by-turn navigation rendering (the
routing/ETA engine is an external dependency here, not designed from
scratch), fraud/fake-GPS detection, driver incentive/bonus programs.

**Back-of-envelope math:**

<KeyStat
  value="~2.5M writes/sec"
  label="driver location pings, one large metro region"
  detail="500,000 active drivers in a metro region, each pinging location every 4 seconds → 500,000 ÷ 4 ≈ 125,000 writes/sec for that one region. A handful of dense metros running concurrently at peak pushes system-wide location writes into the low millions/sec — this is why the requirement targets 'tens of millions of pings/sec system-wide,' aggregated across many independently-scaled regions, not one global counter."
/>

<KeyStat
  value="~800 matches/sec"
  label="ride requests at peak, one dense city"
  detail="A dense city the size of NYC or SF can see on the order of 50,000-70,000 ride requests/hour at evening peak → roughly 50,000 ÷ 3,600 ≈ 14 requests/sec sustained, spiking several times higher (~50-80/sec) around bar close or a big event letting out. Scaled across ride types and a busier global metro, low hundreds to ~800 matching decisions/sec per city at the very peak is the number the matching service has to sustain — small compared to location-ping volume, but each one has a hard few-second latency budget."
/>

The ping-write volume dwarfs the matching-decision volume by roughly
1000:1 — which is exactly why the two are architected as separate
services below: location ingestion is a high-throughput, low-latency
write path into an in-memory index, while matching is a lower-volume,
higher-compute read-and-rank path against that index.

## Deep dives

### Geospatial indexing for driver search

The candidate-set filter that gets "all drivers in the city" down to
"drivers actually worth ranking" before any distance math runs.

<D2Diagram
  title="Geohash vs. H3 hex grid"
  type="architecture"
  chart={`
geohash: Geohash cell (square) {
  style.fill: "#b8722a"
  style.font-color: "#ffffff"
}

  style.fill: "#b8722a"
  style.font-color: "#ffffff"
}
gnote: "8 neighbors, but corner\\nneighbors are ~1.4x farther\\nthan edge neighbors" {
  style.fill: "#4b5262"
  style.font-color: "#ffffff"
}
hnote: "6 neighbors, all at\\nequal distance from center" {
  style.fill: "#4b5262"
  style.font-color: "#ffffff"
}

geohash -> gnote
h3 -> hnote
`}
/>

- **Geohash**: encodes lat/lon into a base-32 string; shared prefix length
  = shared bounding box. Cheap to compute and index (it's just a string
  prefix), but a rider standing 5 meters from a cell boundary has nearby
  drivers sitting in the *adjacent* cell that a naive "query my own cell"
  lookup misses entirely — the **boundary-edge problem**. The fix is
  always querying the cell plus its 8 immediate neighbors, never the exact
  cell alone.
- **Quadtree**: recursively subdivides space into 4 quadrants, going
  deeper where driver density is higher. Naturally density-adaptive — a
  sparse suburb gets large cells, a dense downtown gets small ones — but
  it's a mutable tree structure under constant rebalancing as drivers
  move, which adds real implementation and locking complexity at write
  volume.
- **H3 (hexagonal hierarchical index)**: tiles the world in hexagons at
  multiple resolutions. A hexagon has exactly 6 neighbors, **all at equal
  distance from the center cell** — unlike a square grid, where the 4
  edge-adjacent neighbors are closer than the 4 corner-adjacent ones. That
  uniformity means "expand outward by one ring" behaves consistently in
  every direction, which is what the radius-expansion search below
  depends on. This is why Uber built and open-sourced H3 for exactly this
  problem.

<QuizItem
  question="Why does a square geohash grid distort neighbor distance in a way H3's hexagons don't?"
  answer="A square cell has 8 neighbors, but only 4 share an edge — the other 4 share just a corner and sit roughly 1.4x (√2) farther from the center. A hexagon's 6 neighbors all share a full edge and sit at equal distance, so a 'ring of neighbors' search behaves the same in every direction instead of favoring some directions over others."
/>

### Nearest-driver search / radius expansion

<D2Diagram
  title="Ring-by-ring candidate expansion"
  type="sequence"
  chart={`
shape: sequence_diagram
match: Matching Service
idx: Geospatial Index

match -> idx: "query rider's H3 cell (ring 0)"
idx -> match: "1 driver found"
match -> idx: "expand: query ring 1 (6 neighbor cells)"
idx -> match: "9 drivers found (10 total)"
match -> match: "rank 10 candidates by real ETA"
`}
/>

*The service never queries "all drivers in the city" — it starts at the
rider's own cell and expands outward one ring at a time only until it has
enough candidates, then does the expensive real-distance/ETA computation
on that small set, not the whole city.*

- Start at the rider's H3 cell (or geohash cell); if it has too few
  available drivers (e.g. fewer than ~10), expand to the ring of
  immediate neighbors, then the next ring, stopping as soon as a
  target candidate count is reached.
- **Why not rank by raw cell distance and stop there:** cell adjacency is
  a cheap pre-filter, not the final ranking — actual road distance/ETA
  from the routing engine is what determines who's really "nearest,"
  since a driver one cell away on the wrong side of a river can be
  farther in real travel time than one two cells away with a direct
  route.
- **Sparse-area edge case:** in a low-density suburb, ring expansion might
  need to go 3-4 rings out before finding any driver at all — the search
  radius is adaptive, not fixed, precisely because density varies by
  neighborhood and time of day.

<QuizItem
  question="Why does the matching service rank candidates by real ETA from a routing engine instead of just picking the driver in the nearest H3 cell?"
  answer="Cell adjacency only approximates straight-line proximity, not actual travel time — a driver in the same cell might be on the far side of a river or a highway with no direct route, while a driver one ring out might have a faster real path. Cell-ring expansion is a cheap way to build a small candidate set fast; real ETA ranking is what actually orders them."
/>

### Matching strategy: greedy vs. batched

<D2Diagram
  title="Greedy vs. batched matching window"
  type="architecture"
  chart={`
greedy: Greedy {
  style.fill: "#0e7c86"
  style.font-color: "#ffffff"
}
greedy_out: "assign nearest available\\ndriver immediately" {
  style.fill: "#4b5262"
  style.font-color: "#ffffff"
}
batch: Batched window {
  style.fill: "#0e7c86"
  style.font-color: "#ffffff"
}
batch_out: "collect N requests + drivers\\nover ~2-3s, solve as one\\nbipartite matching problem" {
  style.fill: "#4b5262"
  style.font-color: "#ffffff"
}

greedy -> greedy_out
batch -> batch_out
`}
/>

- **Greedy (assign-first-available)**: as soon as a request arrives, rank
  nearby candidates and offer to the top one. Lowest possible latency per
  request, but locally optimal only — it can assign the closest driver to
  rider A a moment before a slightly-farther-but-actually-better-overall
  assignment would have served both A and a rider B who requests one
  second later.
- **Batched matching window**: hold incoming requests and available
  drivers for a short window (a few seconds), then solve a bipartite
  matching problem over the whole batch at once (e.g. minimizing total
  pickup distance across all pairs, a Hungarian-algorithm-style
  assignment). Produces better aggregate outcomes — fewer total miles
  driven empty, fairer distribution — at the cost of every rider in the
  batch waiting for the window to close before getting an answer.
- **What real systems do**: a short batching window (on the order of a
  few seconds) in dense areas where enough concurrent requests arrive to
  make batch optimization worthwhile, falling back toward greedy
  assignment in sparse areas or off-peak periods where there's rarely more
  than one request to batch with anyway.

<QuizItem
  question="In a sparse suburb at 2am with one ride request every few minutes, does batched matching still make sense?"
  answer="No — batching only pays off when there are multiple concurrent requests and drivers to jointly optimize over. With effectively one request at a time, the batch window just adds latency with nothing to optimize against, so greedy assignment is strictly better in that regime."
/>

### Driver acceptance / reassignment flow

<D2Diagram
  title="Offer cascade on timeout or reject"
  type="state"
  chart={`

  style.fill: "#4b5262"
  style.font-color: "#ffffff"
}
offered_a: Offer sent to Driver A {
  style.fill: "#0e7c86"
  style.font-color: "#ffffff"
}
accepted: Trip matched {
  style.fill: "#0e7c86"
  style.font-color: "#ffffff"
}
offered_b: Offer sent to Driver B (next ranked) {
  style.fill: "#0e7c86"
  style.font-color: "#ffffff"
}
requeued: Request re-enters candidate search {
  style.fill: "#4b5262"
  style.font-color: "#ffffff"
}

ranked -> offered_a
offered_a -> accepted: "accepts within 12s"
offered_a -> offered_b: "timeout or reject"
offered_b -> accepted: "accepts within 12s"
offered_b -> requeued: "timeout or reject,\\nno more ranked candidates nearby"
`}
/>

- Offer goes to exactly one driver at a time, never broadcast to several
  at once — broadcasting risks two drivers both tapping "accept" in the
  same instant, which just moves the double-dispatch race earlier instead
  of avoiding it.
- **Bounded timeout** (commonly ~10-15s): long enough for a driver to
  glance at pickup distance and fare before deciding, short enough that a
  slow-to-respond driver doesn't stall the rider's whole match.
- On timeout or explicit reject, the offer **cascades** to the
  next-ranked candidate from the same candidate set — no need to re-query
  the geospatial index if the list still has unexhausted candidates.
- If the ranked list is exhausted (everyone nearby rejected or timed
  out), the request re-enters the search — either a fresh candidate query
  against a possibly-changed driver set, or back into the next batching
  window.

<QuizItem
  question="Why does an offer go to one driver at a time instead of broadcasting to the top 3 candidates simultaneously and taking whichever accepts first?"
  answer="Broadcasting to multiple drivers means more than one could accept before the system reacts, recreating the double-dispatch problem the claim step is supposed to prevent — it would just move the race from 'who gets offered' to 'who gets un-offered after accepting,' which is a worse user experience for the rejected driver."
/>

### Surge / dynamic pricing

<D2Diagram
  title="Surge multiplier feedback loop"
  type="architecture"
  chart={`
demand: Open ride requests\\n(per H3 cell, rolling window) {
  style.fill: "#4b5262"
  style.font-color: "#ffffff"
}
supply: Available drivers\\n(per H3 cell, rolling window) {
  style.fill: "#4b5262"
  style.font-color: "#ffffff"
}
price: Pricing Service {
  style.fill: "#0e7c86"
  style.font-color: "#ffffff"
}
fare: "base fare x multiplier" {
  style.fill: "#b8722a"
  style.font-color: "#ffffff"
}

demand -> price: "requests/min"
supply -> price: "drivers available"
price -> fare: "ratio -> multiplier,\\ndecays toward 1.0x as\\nsupply catches up"
`}
/>

- Computed **per geo-cell**, not citywide — a stadium letting out spikes
  demand in one H3 cell while the rest of the city is normal, and a
  citywide average would wash that out and under-price exactly the area
  that needs the price signal to attract drivers.
- Multiplier = a function of the open-requests-to-available-drivers ratio
  in that cell over a rolling window (e.g. the last 2-3 minutes), not the
  instantaneous ratio — smoothing avoids reacting to a single noisy
  moment (one request arriving 100ms before another).
- **Decay/cooldown, not a hard reset**: once supply catches up, the
  multiplier ramps back down gradually rather than snapping to 1.0x
  instantly — an abrupt drop would itself create a demand spike (everyone
  who was waiting out the surge requests at once) that recreates the
  imbalance the multiplier just fixed. This is the same oscillation
  problem autoscalers solve with cooldown windows, applied to price
  instead of instance count.
- Surge is shown to the rider **before** they confirm, as a fare quote
  from the pricing service — matching and pricing are separate calls so a
  fare can be shown and locked before a driver is actually assigned.

<QuizItem
  question="Why does the surge multiplier decay gradually back to baseline instead of snapping back to 1.0x the instant supply catches up?"
  answer="An instant snap-back would itself trigger a demand spike — riders who were waiting out the high price would all request at once — recreating the same supply/demand imbalance the multiplier had just corrected. A gradual decay avoids that oscillation, the same way cooldown windows prevent autoscaler flapping."
/>

### Trip lifecycle / state propagation

The trip's state is the single source of truth both apps render from and
every service reacts to — matching, notification, and billing all key off
of it rather than each maintaining their own notion of "where is this
trip right now." Full transition set (including cancellation branches) is
in the worked example below.

### Live tracking / ETA during an active trip

- **Pre-match search pings** (covered above) are index-based: the
  location service upserts the driver's position into the geospatial
  index, and matching reads from that index on demand. No one is
  "watching" any single driver.
- **In-trip pings** are push-based instead: once a trip is matched, the
  driver's location updates are streamed directly to the two parties who
  actually need them (the matched rider, and the rider's app showing
  driver-to-pickup ETA) via a persistent connection (WebSocket or
  long-lived push), not through the shared geospatial index.
- **Why the split**: pushing every driver's every ping to every nearby
  rider's screen doesn't scale — most riders don't care about most
  drivers. Once a trip exists, there are exactly two interested parties,
  so a targeted push is cheap; before a trip exists, the index-based pull
  model is what lets matching query "who's nearby" without a live
  subscription per rider.

<QuizItem
  question="Why do in-trip location updates use a different delivery mechanism (push to two parties) than pre-match search (index reads)?"
  answer="Before a match, matching needs to query 'who's near this rider' on demand across potentially many drivers — an index read model fits that. Once matched, there are exactly two interested parties (this rider, this driver) who need every update immediately, so a targeted push connection is both simpler and cheaper than having them poll or query an index for a driver they're already locked to."
/>

- **Push vs. pull for offer delivery and location**: offers are pushed to
  the driver app (a driver shouldn't have to poll to find out they got a
  ride) via the same persistent connection used for in-trip tracking;
  pre-match location updates are pull-style from matching's point of
  view — matching queries the index when it needs candidates, rather than
  every driver's ping fanning out to every nearby matching request. Push
  fits "exactly one recipient needs this immediately"; pull fits "many
  possible readers, most of whom don't need this specific update at all."

<QuizItem
  question="Why is the driver-claim step held to strong consistency while driver location is allowed to be eventually consistent, in the same system?"
  answer="They have different recoverable-error costs: a slightly stale driver position just means the next ranking pass corrects itself, but a double-dispatched driver (assigned to two trips at once) is a real-world conflict that can't be quietly fixed after the fact — one rider is left stranded. The invariant that actually needs strong consistency is narrow (one claim, one driver), so only that step pays the consistency cost."
/>

## Worked example: one ride request end-to-end

Priya opens the app in a dense downtown area at 6:15pm and requests a
ride to the airport. Trace what happens from request to fare.

<D2Diagram

  type="sequence"
  chart={`

Priya's request lands at the matching service, which queries her H3 cell
plus its immediate ring and gets back 14 nearby candidates. It ranks them
by real ETA, not raw distance, and Driver A comes out on top — three
minutes away. In parallel, the pricing service returns a fare quote:
$18 base fare with a 1.2x surge multiplier applied for this cell right
now, for $21.60. Driver A's offer times out after 12 seconds with no
response — maybe they're mid-conversation with a previous rider — so the
offer cascades to Driver B, next on the ranked list, who accepts within
four seconds. The trip service creates the trip record, atomically claims
Driver B (so no other rider's matching pass can also claim him), and both
Priya's and Driver B's apps switch to live push-based tracking. Once
Driver B picks Priya up and the trip actually begins, the fare locks in
at the $21.60 quoted — later demand changes in that cell no longer affect
this already-matched trip.

enroute -> inprogress: "driver arrives, rider picked up"
enroute -> cancelled: "rider or driver cancels\\n(cancellation fee may apply)"
inprogress -> completed: "drop-off confirmed"
inprogress -> cancelled: "trip aborted mid-ride\\n(rare — safety/emergency)"
`}
/>

*Cancellation is reachable from every pre-completion state, by either
party, not just as a single "cancelled" branch off the happy path — a
rider can cancel before pickup, a driver can cancel after accepting but
before arriving, and (rarely) a trip can be aborted mid-ride.*

<QuizItem
  question="In the worked example, why does the fare stay at $21.60 even if the surge multiplier in that cell changes between matching and pickup?"
  answer="The fare quote is locked in at the point of matching, tied to the specific trip, not recomputed live off the current cell multiplier — otherwise a rider's price could change mid-negotiation for reasons entirely outside their control, and a driver couldn't reliably know what a trip pays before accepting it."
/>

## Interview angle

**"How do you prevent two riders from being matched to the same driver at
the same time?"** The offer/accept step needs an atomic claim, the same
shape as Ticketmaster's seat hold: when a driver accepts, the trip service
does a conditional write — e.g. `SET driver:B:status trip_id NX` or a
DB row update guarded by `WHERE status = 'available'` — so only the first
accepting write wins and a concurrent second claim attempt visibly fails
rather than silently overwriting. Combined with sending only one offer at
a time (covered above), this makes double-dispatch structurally
prevented, not just unlikely.

**"How does the design change for scheduled/advance-booked rides?"** A
scheduled ride can't run through live radius-expansion search hours
before the trip — instead it's stored as a future intent (pickup time,
location) and a separate scheduler wakes it up shortly before the pickup
window, at which point it re-enters the same on-demand matching flow used
here. The extra design surface is making sure enough drivers are likely
to be available at that future time and place (a supply-forecasting
concern, not a matching-algorithm concern) and handling a "no driver
found" fallback gracefully since there's a promise already made to the
rider.

**"How would you extend this to pooled/shared rides?"** Pooling turns a
single rider-to-driver match into a small route-optimization problem: the
matching step now has to find a driver whose current route can absorb an
additional pickup/drop-off pair without excessive detour for existing
passengers, and the trip's state machine needs multiple concurrent
rider legs instead of one. Practically this means adding a
route-re-optimization step after candidate ranking — given a driver
already mid-route, does inserting this new pickup/drop-off pair keep
everyone's added detour under a threshold — rather than a new matching
algorithm from scratch.

### Recap quiz

<QuizItem
  question="Why does a naive 'scan every driver and compute distance' approach fail at Uber's scale, even with a fast database?"
  answer="Distance computation against every driver in a city is O(n) work per request, and the driver table itself is being rewritten every few seconds by every driver's location ping — the bottleneck isn't computing one distance, it's the sheer number of drivers to check per request, which a geospatial index avoids by pre-narrowing to a small nearby candidate set."
/>

<QuizItem
  question="What specific property of H3's hexagonal cells makes ring-expansion search behave more predictably than a geohash's square cells?"
  answer="A hexagon has 6 neighbors, all sharing a full edge and sitting at equal distance from the center. A square has 8 neighbors, but the 4 corner-adjacent ones are about 1.4x farther than the 4 edge-adjacent ones, so 'expand outward by one ring' is distance-uniform in H3 but distorted in a square grid."
/>

<QuizItem
  question="When is batched-window matching worth its added latency compared to greedy assignment?"
  answer="Only when there are enough concurrent requests and drivers in the same area to actually optimize an assignment across — dense areas at peak. In sparse areas or off-peak times, with rarely more than one request to batch with, the batch window just adds delay with nothing to jointly optimize."
/>

<QuizItem
  question="What triggers a surge multiplier to rise in a given area, and why is it computed per-cell rather than citywide?"
  answer="It rises when the ratio of open ride requests to available drivers in that cell, over a rolling window, climbs — e.g. a stadium letting out. Computing it per-cell instead of citywide keeps the signal local to where supply is actually short; a citywide average would dilute a sharp local spike and under-price exactly the area that needs it."
/>

<QuizItem
  question="Why does the surge multiplier decay gradually instead of resetting to 1.0x the moment supply and demand rebalance?"
  answer="An instant reset would itself cause a demand spike — riders who'd been waiting out the higher price would all request at once — recreating the same imbalance the multiplier had just corrected. A gradual decay avoids that oscillation."
/>

<QuizItem
  question="Driver location updates are eventually consistent, but claiming a driver for a trip must be strongly consistent. Why isn't the whole system just built one way or the other?"
  answer="They protect against different failure costs: stale location just means a match might briefly target a driver who moved slightly (self-correcting on the next search pass), while a double-claimed driver is an unrecoverable real-world conflict. Applying strong consistency everywhere would bottleneck the tens-of-millions-of-pings/sec location path for no benefit; applying eventual consistency to the claim step would allow double-dispatch."
/>

<QuizItem
  question="An offer to Driver A times out with no response. What happens next, and why doesn't the system re-query the geospatial index immediately?"
  answer="The offer cascades to the next-ranked candidate (Driver B) from the same already-computed candidate list, because that list is still valid for the few seconds this took — re-querying the index for every single timeout would add latency without meaningfully changing the nearby-driver set. The index is only re-queried if the whole ranked list is exhausted."
/>

### Open design challenge

**Extend the matching service to support driver preferences (e.g. a
driver only wants airport trips) without breaking the nearest-match
latency guarantee.** The current design ranks candidates purely by
ETA/distance. Now some drivers want to opt into filters — only airport
trips, only trips under 15 minutes, no pooled rides — and the match
should respect those without re-scanning drivers one by one or blowing
the ~2-5s latency budget. How do you represent a driver's preferences so
they can be checked cheaply during ranking? Where does the filtering step
happen relative to the ring-expansion candidate search? What happens if a
rider's request matches no preference-compatible driver nearby, but does
match drivers who'd normally be filtered out?

<details>
<summary>Reference approach (click to reveal)</summary>

- **Represent preferences as a cheap filter, not a fetch.** Store each
  online driver's active preference flags (e.g. a small bitmask or a set
  of tags: `airport_only`, `max_trip_15m`, `no_pool`) alongside their
  entry in the geospatial index itself, not in a separate lookup — so
  checking a candidate's preferences during ranking costs an in-memory
  field read, not a second network round trip per candidate.
- **Filter after candidate generation, not before.** Ring-expansion
  search still runs unchanged to build the ~10-20 nearest candidates by
  distance; preference filtering happens as a cheap in-memory pass over
  that already-small set, discarding incompatible drivers before the
  real-ETA ranking step. Filtering the index itself per-request (e.g. "only
  index airport-only drivers for this query") would need a differently
  partitioned index per preference combination, which doesn't scale to
  arbitrary preference combinations.
- **If the request needs a specific ride attribute** (e.g. the rider's
  destination actually is the airport), tag the request the same way and
  only match against drivers whose preference set is compatible —
  equivalent to a set-intersection check per candidate, still O(candidate
  count), not O(all drivers).
- **Handling the "no compatible driver nearby" case**: expand the ring
  search further before falling back — the preference filter reduces the
  candidate pool, so the system may need to search a slightly wider
  radius than a preference-free match would. If still no match, degrade
  explicitly: either widen further with a longer quoted ETA, or (product
  decision, not an architectural one) offer the rider a match with a
  driver whose preference doesn't strictly apply if none of the driver's
  preferences veto it outright — the design surfaces the trade-off rather
  than silently picking one.
- **Preferences don't change the O(n) shape of the hot path.** Because
  filtering happens over the small ranked candidate set, not over the
  full driver population, the ring-expansion search stays the dominant
  cost and the latency budget is preserved — preference matching adds a
  constant-time pass over a set that's already small by construction.

</details>
