title: "Netflix: High-Level Design"

Netflix's hard problem isn't storing movies — it's that "press play" has
to turn into a stream of bytes arriving faster than the screen consumes
them, for hundreds of millions of people at once, on every kind of
network from gigabit fiber to a congested mobile tower, without ever
hitting a central server hard enough to notice.

## Problem framing

Video-on-demand at global scale is really two coupled problems wearing
one UI:

- **Getting bytes to the user fast and cheaply** — delivery: encoding,
  segmenting, and a content-delivery network (CDN) that keeps the actual
  video bytes physically close to the viewer.
- **Getting the *right* bytes in front of the user** — catalog and
  recommendations: knowing what exists, what's licensed where, and what
  a given profile is likely to want to watch next.

Most of this lesson follows that split, because the two problems have
almost opposite engineering shapes.

{/* concept-dependency: HLD-01 not yet built, explained inline */}
Netflix's traffic pattern makes delivery the harder half. A playback
session is **long-lived** (a two-hour film, not a 200ms API call),
**resumable** (pausing and re-fetching from where you left off is
normal, not an error case), and **progressively consumed** (the client
only ever needs the *next* few seconds of video, not the whole file at
once). Layer on a **highly skewed popularity distribution** — a new
season of a hit show can account for a large share of an evening's total
traffic, while most of the back catalog is watched rarely — and the
dominant cost driver becomes bandwidth, not storage or database
throughput. That's a different shape from, say, a social feed, where the
hard problem is fanning out many small, unpredictable writes to many
followers under ordering and consistency constraints. Here the write
side (new titles published, viewing events recorded) is comparatively
small and slow-changing; the read side is enormous, predictable in
aggregate, and dominated by raw byte volume — which is why this system
is a **caching/CDN problem first**, and a database-scale problem only
second.

<QuizItem
  question="Why is Netflix's traffic pattern more of a CDN/caching problem than a database-scaling problem, unlike a social media feed?"
  answer="Because the dominant cost and traffic driver is streaming large volumes of video bytes for long-lived sessions with a highly skewed, largely predictable popularity distribution — not a high rate of small, unpredictable writes. Getting popular video bytes physically close to viewers (CDN) does more for this system's scale problem than any database optimization could."
/>

## Requirements & capacity estimate

**Functional requirements:**

- Browse/search a personalized content catalog (titles, metadata,
  artwork, episode listings)
- Play a title with adaptive-bitrate streaming — quality shifts with
  available bandwidth, no manual selection required
- Resume playback from the last-watched position, per profile, across
  devices
- Personalized recommendation rows on the home screen ("Because You
  Watched", "Top Picks")
- Studio-to-catalog content pipeline: ingest a source master, encode the
  full bitrate/resolution ladder, publish to the catalog once ready

**Non-functional requirements:**

- Low playback startup latency — low single-digit seconds from pressing
  play to first frame
- Low rebuffer ratio (fraction of playback time stalled waiting for
  data), even on constrained or variable networks
- Global scale: hundreds of millions of concurrent streaming sessions,
  most viewing hours served from near-edge/ISP-embedded caches, not a
  central origin
- High origin cache-hit ratio — the origin (object storage + control
  plane) must not bottleneck popular, predictable-demand content
- Bandwidth-cost awareness — video is the dominant cost driver, so
  encoding efficiency and cache placement both matter economically, not
  just for latency
- Multi-region availability — a regional outage shouldn't take down
  playback globally

**Out of scope for this lesson:** DRM/licensing legal details (Widevine/
FairPlay/PlayReady are named as the mechanism, not explained
cryptographically), the ML ranking model's internals, billing/account
management, and live/linear streaming — this lesson is video-on-demand
only.

<KeyStat
  value="~150-600 Tbps"
  label="aggregate egress bandwidth demanded at global peak"
  detail="~30-120 million concurrent streams (a plausible range for a service with hundreds of millions of subscribers, most active during evening peak) x ~5 Mbps average bitrate (a mix of SD, HD, and 4K sessions) = 150,000,000-600,000,000 Mbps of instantaneous demand. No single data center's network egress — or its transit links to the public internet — is provisioned anywhere near that range, let alone affordable at that scale from commercial CDN pricing. That number is what rules out serving primarily from centralized origin servers and makes an ISP-embedded edge-cache network (the CDN deep dive below) a structural requirement, not an optimization."

architecture — it's why a catalog database having a bad day doesn't
touch anyone's already-playing video.

Encoding a full bitrate ladder for one title is embarrassingly parallel
— each rung (and, with per-shot encoding, each shot within a rung) is an
independent job — so this fans out across many worker machines rather
than encoding rungs sequentially on one box. A title only becomes
visible in the catalog once every rung it needs has finished encoding
and the manifest referencing them has been generated; a partially
encoded title publishing early would let a client request a rung that
doesn't exist yet.

### Encoding & the bitrate ladder

How the ladder itself gets built has three escalating approaches:

- **Fixed ladder** — every title gets the same fixed set of
  resolution/bitrate pairs. Simple, but wasteful: a static talking-head
  drama and a fast-cut action movie don't need the same bits to look
  equally good at a given resolution.
- **Per-title encoding** — analyze each title's visual complexity first
  (motion, detail, noise) and pick the *minimum* bitrate per resolution
  rung that still clears a quality bar, instead of a one-size-fits-all
  number. An animated film with flat colors and little motion can hit
  the same perceived quality at a much lower bitrate than a high-action
  film — Netflix's own published results put per-title savings at
  roughly 15-20% in encoded bits for the same perceptual quality, with
  no visible difference to the viewer.
- **Per-shot (per-scene) encoding** — go finer than per-title: a single
  film has quiet dialogue scenes and explosive action scenes, so encode
  *each shot* at its own optimal point rather than one ladder for the
  whole runtime. This captures savings per-title encoding misses because
  per-title only optimizes at the whole-title granularity.

<Point icon="branch">
  Why animation and a high-action film can't share a ladder: a fixed
  ladder is sized for the hardest case (fast motion, high detail) across
  the whole catalog, so anything simpler than that wastes bits it didn't
  need — multiplied across every playback of every low-complexity title,
  that waste is real money at Netflix's delivery volume.
</Point>

<QuizItem
  question="A fixed bitrate ladder gives a minimalist animated short and a fast-action blockbuster the exact same 1080p bitrate. What's the downside?"
  answer="The animated short is over-encoded — it could hit the same perceived quality at a meaningfully lower bitrate, since it has far less motion/detail complexity. That wasted bitrate costs storage and, more importantly, delivery bandwidth on every single playback, which per-title encoding fixes by picking each title's own minimum sufficient bitrate per rung."
/>

### Segmenting & manifests

Video isn't streamed as one giant file — it's chopped into short
**segments**, typically 2-10 seconds each. Two reasons this matters:

- **Quality switching mid-playback**: if the network gets worse three
  minutes in, the client can start fetching the *next segment* at a
  lower rung without restarting playback or re-downloading anything
  already watched. A single monolithic file would force an all-or-
  nothing quality choice made once, upfront.
- **Fast, resumable startup**: the player only needs the first segment
  before it can start decoding and showing frames — not the whole title.

A **manifest** is the index tying it together: for a given title, it
lists every available bitrate rung and the URLs (or URL templates) for
each rung's segments. Two manifest formats dominate:

In practice a service like Netflix serves both — the manifest format is
chosen per client platform, not once globally, since the two formats
solve the same problem for different device ecosystems rather than
competing head-to-head.

<QuizItem

  answer="Because it lets the client switch quality mid-playback by fetching the next segment at a different bitrate rung, without restarting or re-downloading what's already played. A single unsegmented file would force one quality decision for the entire session."
/>

### CDN edge caching deep dive

This is where the aggregate egress number from the capacity estimate
gets solved. Netflix's real system (Open Connect) runs appliances
physically racked inside ISP networks and internet exchange points — the
video bytes for a popular title are often only one network hop from the
viewer, never crossing the public internet back to a central origin at
all.

*Both diagrams show the same three tiers; the difference is which one

title nobody forecast, or a fresh edge node that hasn't been seeded
yet).*

{/* concept-dependency: HLD-06 not yet built, explained inline */}
Three mechanisms make this tier work, each solving a distinct problem:

- **Proactive/scheduled fill vs. reactive pull-on-miss.** Netflix
  precomputes which titles are likely to be popular in each region over
  the next day (using viewing history and upcoming-release signals) and
  pushes those titles to edge caches during off-peak overnight hours,
  when spare ISP bandwidth is cheap and plentiful. This is viable
  specifically *because* video popularity is predictable in aggregate —
  a new season's premiere or a long-running catalog favorite has a
  forecastable demand curve, unlike a one-off viral social post. Reactive
  pull-on-miss still exists as a fallback for the unpredictable tail, but
  it's the exception, not the steady-state path.
- **Cache invalidation on a new release or a content fix.** A blanket
  cache flush would be catastrophic at this scale — it would force every
  edge node to re-pull from origin simultaneously, recreating exactly
  the thundering-herd problem caching exists to avoid. Instead, content
  versions get their own **versioned cache keys** (e.g. a content hash
  or version suffix baked into the segment/manifest URL) so a corrected
  encode is simply a *new* object at a new URL — old cached copies age
  out normally, and only the specific title that changed gets
  selectively re-filled, not the whole cache.
- **Origin shield / multi-tier caching.** Thousands of edge nodes all
  hitting the origin directly on a miss would still overwhelm it, even
  if each individual node's miss rate is low — the aggregate is the
  problem. A regional shield tier sits between edge nodes and the
  origin: it absorbs and *coalesces* misses from many edge nodes in a
  region into far fewer requests reaching origin, and itself caches the
  result so the second edge node's miss in that region never reaches
  origin at all.

<QuizItem
  question="Why is proactive CDN fill viable for Netflix's video catalog but wouldn't work well for, say, a live social media feed?"
  answer="Because video popularity is forecastable in aggregate — viewing history and release schedules predict which titles will be in demand tomorrow, so pre-filling overnight during cheap off-peak bandwidth pays off. A social feed's content is unpredictable and constantly changing, so there's nothing reliable to forecast and pre-fill."
/>

### Adaptive bitrate algorithm

The client, not the server, decides which bitrate rung to fetch next —
it has the information the server doesn't (its own real-time buffer
state and observed throughput). Two families of decision logic:

<CompareTable
  title="ABR decision strategies"
  rows={[

      approach: "Throughput-based",

      cons: ["Noisy — a single slow segment can trigger an unnecessary quality drop", "Doesn't directly account for how much buffer is already banked"],

      cons: ["Slower to react to a real, sustained bandwidth drop if the buffer is still comfortably full"],
      chosenBecause: "real ABR players blend both signals rather than picking one exclusively",
    },
  ]}
/>

Every few seconds, this loop is balancing one trade-off: pick a rung too
aggressively high and risk a stall (rebuffer) if throughput doesn't hold
up; pick too conservatively low and the viewer gets unnecessarily soft
video despite having bandwidth to spare. Rebuffering is the worse of the
two failure modes from a viewer-experience standpoint — a brief quality
dip is barely noticed, a stall is not — which is why most real ABR
algorithms are deliberately biased to err toward the safer, lower rung at
startup (a low-risk initial choice, stepping up once buffer is banked)
rather than opening at the highest bitrate the current throughput sample
suggests.

<QuizItem
  question="Why do most ABR clients start playback at a conservative, lower bitrate rather than the highest one the initial throughput sample supports?"
  answer="Because a rebuffer (stall) hurts the viewing experience far more than a temporarily softer picture, and the client has no buffer banked yet at startup to absorb a misjudged throughput estimate. Starting low and stepping up once buffer builds trades a small, unnoticed quality cost for protection against an early stall."
/>

### Catalog/metadata service design

The catalog is a **read-heavy, globally replicated** dataset: titles,
artwork variants (different thumbnails per locale/A-B test), episode
listings, and — critically — **regional availability** (a title licensed
in one country may not be licensed in another). It's served from caches
in front of a source-of-truth store, the same read-scaling shape as any
high-traffic, low-write content system.

A playback request resolves through exactly this chain: the client asks
for a title, the catalog service checks the requesting profile's region
against `region_availability`, resolves the right `Asset` (e.g. the
correct dub/subtitle track set), and returns that asset's `Manifest` —
which is what actually contains the CDN URL template the player uses to
start fetching segments. Regional availability is deliberately a
**catalog-service concern**, not a CDN concern: the CDN doesn't know or
care about licensing, it just serves whatever segment URL it's asked
for — access control happens one layer up, before a URL is ever handed
to a client.

{/* concept-dependency: HLD-07 not yet built, explained inline */}
Because reads so heavily outweigh writes here, the source-of-truth store
is typically fronted by read replicas and a caching layer (cache-aside:
check cache, fall back to the store on a miss, populate on the way back)
rather than optimized for write throughput — the opposite bias from the
booking-style write path a case study like Ticketmaster needs.

<QuizItem
  question="Why does the catalog service check region availability rather than the CDN refusing to serve a segment in a region it's not licensed for?"
  answer="Because the CDN's job is pure byte delivery — it has no concept of licensing windows or regional rights. Access control has to happen before a client is ever handed a segment URL, which is why region_availability lives on the Title record the catalog service checks, not as CDN-layer logic."
/>

### Recommendations at a high level

Home-screen personalization splits into two distinct problems solved
differently:

- **Candidate generation** — which titles could plausibly be shown to
  this profile at all (out of the entire catalog)? This narrows a huge
  space down to a manageable shortlist per row/theme.
- **Ranking** — given a shortlist, what order should they appear in for
  *this* profile specifically? This is where per-user personalization
  signal (watch history, ratings, similar-profile behavior) does the
  heavy lifting.

{/* concept-dependency: HLD-05 not yet built, explained inline */}
Most personalized rows are **precomputed offline**: a batch job scores
candidates per profile on a schedule (hourly or nightly, not per
request) and writes the results to a fast-read cache, so opening the app
never waits on a model inference call — it's a cache read, same latency
profile as any other cached page load. Some signal is layered in
**online** for freshness (e.g. re-ranking based on what was just watched
in this session), but the heavy candidate-generation and full-catalog
scoring work stays offline because running it per-request, per-profile,
against the whole catalog would be far too slow and expensive to serve
on the home-screen's latency budget.

**"Continue Watching" is a different, simpler code path** on purpose: it
isn't ML-ranked at all, just a **recency-ordered list** of titles with a
non-null resume position for that profile, event-driven off the same
watch-progress writes that power the resume-playback feature itself. It
doesn't need candidate generation or ranking because the ordering
question ("what did I watch most recently") is already fully answered
by a timestamp, not a model.

<QuizItem
  question="Why is 'Continue Watching' not part of the ML-ranked recommendation pipeline?"
  answer="Because its ordering (most recently watched first) is a simple, deterministic fact already captured by the resume-position timestamp — there's no candidate-generation or ranking problem to solve, so routing it through the recommendation pipeline would just add latency and complexity for no benefit."
/>

## Worked example: one playback session, start to mid-session upshift

A viewer on a moderately congested home Wi-Fi network presses play on an
episode. Trace what happens from that click to the client stepping up to
a higher-quality segment once the network turns out better than the
opening estimate.

<D2Diagram
  title="Playback session: manifest resolution through mid-session bitrate upshift"
  type="sequence"
  chart={`

The client never asks the streaming service for permission to change
quality mid-playback — it just requests a different rung's segment URL
from the same manifest it already has. That's the entire point of the
manifest holding every rung's URLs upfront: a quality change is a
client-local decision followed by an ordinary CDN fetch, not a new round
trip to any backend service. Both segment fetches above hit the edge
cache directly — the streaming and catalog services are only involved
once, at the very start, to resolve which manifest to use; they're off
the critical path for every segment fetch that follows.

## Interview angle

**"A title just released and everyone wants it at once — how does the
CDN avoid a thundering-herd miss storm on the origin?"** This is exactly
what proactive fill exists to prevent: a hotly anticipated release is a
*forecastable* spike, so it's pre-pushed to edge nodes ahead of its
release time, meaning the vast majority of day-one requests are cache
hits from the start rather than misses. For the residual unpredictable
tail (a smaller title unexpectedly going viral), the origin-shield tier
coalesces concurrent misses from many edge nodes into far fewer origin
requests, so even an imperfect forecast doesn't translate into origin
load scaling with edge-node count.

**"The user's network drops mid-playback — walk through what the client
and server each do."** The client's buffer/throughput monitor detects
the throughput collapse (or outright fetch failures) and its ABR logic
immediately steps down to a lower, safer bitrate rung — or, if the
connection is fully gone, keeps retrying the current segment against the
buffered playback runway it has left before the screen has to show a
spinner. On the server side, nothing proactively "knows" the user
dropped — there's no persistent connection to lose. The edge cache and
streaming service are stateless with respect to any one session; the
next request that does arrive (a retry, or a resumed connection) is
served exactly like any other segment fetch, with no reconnection
handshake beyond ordinary HTTP.

**"How would you extend this to support live/linear content?"** This
probes exactly which VOD assumptions break. Segments don't exist ahead
of time for a live stream — they're produced continuously as the
broadcast happens, so proactive fill (which assumes the content already
exists to be pushed) doesn't apply; instead, each new segment has to
propagate edge-ward within a couple of seconds of being encoded, a much
tighter latency budget than VOD's cache-ahead-of-demand model. The
manifest also becomes a live, growing document instead of a fixed one,
and ABR's buffer-based strategies need adjusting since a live stream
can't let the client buffer arbitrarily far ahead without introducing
broadcast delay.

### Open design challenge

**Design the resume-playback ("Continue Watching") feature so it works
correctly across devices with the least possible write latency.** A
viewer pauses a show on their phone at the 14-minute mark, then opens a
smart TV app for the same profile a minute later and expects it to offer
resuming from roughly that same point — without playback feeling laggy
on the device that's actively writing progress. What data model captures
watch position per profile per title? How often should the playing
device write progress, and does every write need to be durable
immediately, or can some be batched? What happens if the same profile is
watching the same title simultaneously on two devices (a shared family
profile) — whose position wins? How does a device know a fresher
position exists somewhere else when it opens the app?

<details>
<summary>Reference approach (click to reveal)</summary>

- **Data model**: a `WatchProgress` record keyed by `(profile_id,
  title_id)` — not per-device — storing `position_seconds`,
  `updated_at`, and the `device_id` that made the last write. Keying by
  profile+title (not profile+title+device) is what makes cross-device
  resume automatic: there's exactly one current position per title per
  profile to read, not one per device to merge.
- **Write frequency and durability**: the playing device writes progress
  on a short interval (e.g. every 10-30 seconds) plus on pause/stop/app-
  background — not on every frame. These periodic writes don't need to
  be synchronously durable on the critical path of playback itself (a
  lost update just means resuming a few seconds earlier than ideal, a
  minor annoyance, not a correctness bug); the pause/stop write is the
  one worth an extra effort to confirm succeeded, since that's the
  position the viewer actually expects to see next time.
- **Concurrent writers (shared profile, two devices)**: use **last-
  write-wins by `updated_at`** as the default resolution — simple, and
  the failure mode (briefly showing the wrong device's position) is
  low-stakes and self-correcting the next time either device writes
  again. A more defensive variant tracks progress *per device*
  internally but only surfaces the single most-recently-updated one as
  "Continue Watching," so a still-active second device's position isn't
  silently discarded, just not shown as primary until it becomes the
  most recent write.
- **Cross-device freshness**: don't have devices poll for a fresher
  position. Read the current `WatchProgress` row on app open/resume (a
  fast point read keyed by profile+title, not a scan), which is cheap
  and already correct by construction since there's one authoritative
  row per profile+title — no cache invalidation or push mechanism is
  needed just to answer "where did I leave off," only a low-latency read
  path in front of the store, the same cache-aside shape as the rest of
  the read-heavy metadata surface in this lesson.

</details>
