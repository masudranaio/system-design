---
title: "Food Panda: High-Level Design"
---

A ride-share match is instant: rider and driver both exist right now, so
the system just has to find the closest pair. A food-delivery match can't
be instant, because one side of the match — the meal — doesn't exist yet.
Food Panda's hard problem is coordinating three parties who don't share a
database, around an order whose "ready" time is a kitchen's best guess,
not a fact the system controls.

## Problem framing

{/* concept-dependency: HLD-08 not yet built, explained inline */}
This is a **three-sided marketplace**: customer, restaurant, and courier,
each running their own app, each trusting their own clock. Contrast this
with Ride-Sharing (CS-09), a **two-sided** marketplace — rider and driver
are both available the instant a trip request fires, so matching is a
pure nearest-neighbor search. Food Panda's third side, the restaurant,
inserts a **production step** between "order placed" and "ready to
match": food has to be cooked. A courier assigned too early stands
around a hot kitchen; a courier assigned too late leaves the food
cooling on the counter. Matching timing, not just matching distance, is
the defining problem.

A second contrast worth naming up front, against Ticketmaster (CS-08):
Ticketmaster's hard problem is *contention* — many buyers racing for one
scarce seat, solved with locking. Food Panda's three parties don't
contend for the same resource at all — nobody is racing to "buy" the
order. Each party mutates their own view of it (restaurant marks it
`preparing`, courier marks it `picked_up`) without ever touching the
same row the others touch. The interesting problem here is **event
ordering and fan-out notification**, not locking — covered in its own
deep dive below.

<Point icon="users">Three independent actors, no shared database, no
mutual trust in each other's clocks or state.</Point>
<Point icon="clock">A real-world production step (cooking) the system
can only estimate, never control.</Point>
<Point icon="branch">No resource contention between the three parties —
unlike Ticketmaster's seat inventory, nobody is racing to lock the same
row.</Point>

<QuizItem
  question="Why can't Food Panda match a courier to an order the instant it's placed, the way Ride-Sharing matches a driver to a rider request?"
  answer="Because the order isn't ready to be picked up yet — the restaurant still has to cook it. Matching too early leaves a courier waiting at the restaurant; the system has to time the match against a prep-time estimate, not just against courier proximity."
/>

## Requirements & capacity estimate

**Functional requirements:**

- Browse/search restaurants by location, cuisine, and availability
- View a restaurant's menu and place an order (cart → checkout)
- Restaurant accepts or rejects the order, then reports prep progress
  (received → preparing → ready for pickup)
- System matches the order to an available courier and offers the
  assignment (accept/decline with timeout)
- Courier picks up and delivers the order; live location is visible to
  the customer throughout
- Delivery is confirmed (by customer or courier)
- Cancellation path for customer, restaurant, or courier, gated to the
  lifecycle stages where each is actually allowed to cancel

**Non-functional requirements:**

- Matching latency: courier assigned within a few seconds of an order
  going ready-soon, not after the food is already cold
- Location-update throughput: tens of thousands of courier GPS pings/sec
  citywide at peak, delivered to trackers within a few seconds
- ETA accuracy: a live countdown that's usefully close and recalculates
  smoothly, not exact and not jumpy
- High availability through 5-10x baseline order volume at lunch/dinner
  rushes
- Eventual consistency across the three parties' views of one order is
  acceptable — explicitly not a strict-consistency problem the way seat
  inventory is

**Out of scope:** restaurant menu/inventory internals, payment
authorization/settlement details, courier onboarding and pay structure,
search ranking, in-app chat.

## Deep dives

### Courier-order matching

{/* concept-dependency: HLD-08 not yet built, explained inline */}
"Assign the nearest courier" is the naive answer; real systems weigh
several strategies against each other, because nearest-first can leave a
slightly-farther courier idle while a closer one is about to become busy
elsewhere.

<CompareTable
  title="Courier-order matching strategies"
  rows={[
    {
      approach: "Greedy nearest-available",
      pros: ["Simplest to implement", "Lowest matching latency — decide the instant a candidate is found"],
      cons: ["Locally optimal only — can strand a courier 2 blocks away while a better global assignment existed", "No lookahead across simultaneously-ready orders"],
    },
    {
      approach: "Batch-window assignment",
      pros: ["Solves a small optimization problem over N orders and M couriers together — better overall efficiency and fairness", "Reduces total courier travel distance across the batch"],
      cons: ["Adds a deliberate wait (a few seconds) before dispatching, which costs matching latency", "More complex — needs a solver, not just a nearest-neighbor query"],
      chosenBecause: "Food Panda's real dispatch systems (this pattern is documented in DoorDash's public engineering writeups) run this on a short interval per geographic zone, trading a few seconds of latency for meaningfully better courier utilization",
    },
    {
      approach: "Offer/accept with timeout",
      pros: ["Respects courier autonomy — a courier can decline an offer they don't want", "Naturally handles a courier going unresponsive: timeout triggers re-offer without a special-case code path"],
      cons: ["Adds latency per rejected/timed-out offer before the next candidate is tried", "Needs a re-offer/escalation policy so a bad batch of declines doesn't stall an order indefinitely"],
    },
  ]}
/>

These aren't mutually exclusive: a batch-window solver picks the
*candidate* courier for each order, and that candidate is still offered
the assignment with a short accept/decline timeout (typically 15-30
seconds) rather than force-assigned — combining the second and third
rows.

Nearest-courier candidate search itself — "which couriers are within 2km
of this restaurant right now" — uses the same geo-indexing primitive
Ride-Sharing's matching lesson covers for rider/driver search
(geohash/quadtree/H3-grid proximity lookups); that mechanism doesn't
change here, only what's done with the candidate set once it's found.

<QuizItem
  question="Why might a batch-window matching approach out-perform greedy nearest-available, even though it adds a few seconds of latency?"
  answer="Greedy assignment locks in a courier the instant a candidate is found, which can strand a nearby courier for a slightly-later order while a farther courier gets sent on a longer trip. A batch window collects several orders and couriers together and solves them jointly, reducing total travel distance and improving fairness across the batch — at the cost of a short deliberate delay before dispatching."
/>

### Order batching

A courier carrying one order at a time is safe but inefficient — most of
a delivery trip is driving, not carrying food. Batching lets one courier
serve multiple orders in a single trip.

- **Same-restaurant batching** — two orders from the same restaurant,
  picked up together, delivered on one loop. Lowest risk: both orders
  came off the same kitchen at roughly the same time, so neither sits
  much longer than a single-order trip would have taken.
- **Route-batching** — orders from different nearby restaurants,
  combined because their pickup and drop-off points overlap well enough
  that one courier's loop covers both without a large detour for either.
  Higher courier utilization, but the second order's food waits longer
  in the courier's bag while the first is delivered.
- **When *not* to batch** — perishable/hot food with a short
  quality window, and orders with a tight promised ETA the platform has
  already committed to the customer. The matching service checks a
  batch candidate's added detour time against the second order's
  remaining ETA budget before combining; if adding a stop would blow
  through that budget, it dispatches the order solo instead.

<Point icon="clock">Batching trades delivery speed for courier
utilization and per-order delivery cost — it's a knob, not a default.</Point>

<QuizItem
  question="A courier already has one hot pizza order in the bag. The matching service is deciding whether to add a second order from a different restaurant 8 minutes out of the way. What should it check before batching them?"
  answer="Whether the 8-minute detour still fits inside both orders' remaining ETA budget and quality window — pizza degrades fast, so an 8-minute delay may already blow the promised ETA or food-quality bar for the first order. If it does, the service should dispatch the second order to a different courier instead of batching."
/>

### Live location tracking

{/* concept-dependency: HLD-08 not yet built, explained inline */}
Two questions here are distinct even though they sound like one: how a
courier's phone *gets* its location to the server, and how the geo-index
that answers "which couriers are near X" is structured. Ride-Sharing's
matching lesson covers the geo-index choice (Redis geospatial commands
vs. a quadtree/H3-grid) in depth for the driver-search case — that
choice is unchanged here, so this lesson doesn't re-derive it.

      pros: ["Simpler server model — no persistent connections to track", "Easier to reason about under load — every request is independent"],

/>

What's genuinely new relative to Ride-Sharing's single-rider tracking
case: one courier's location update has to **fan out to every customer
currently tracking one of that courier's active orders** — up to a
courier's batch size at once, not just one subscriber. The fan-out
service (diagram above) keeps a subscription map (`courier_id →
[order_id...] → [customer_connection...]`) and pushes each location
update to all of them, rather than making each customer's client poll
the geo-store directly.

<QuizItem

  answer="Both customers tracking those two orders — the fan-out service looks up all order subscriptions tied to that courier_id and pushes the update to each customer's live connection (WebSocket/SSE), rather than each customer separately polling the geo-store."
/>

### ETA prediction

{/* concept-dependency: HLD-08 not yet built, explained inline */}
A single "12 minutes" estimate hides three genuinely different
predictions chained together — treating ETA as a pipeline, not one
number, is what makes it possible to improve or debug any one stage
independently.

1. **Kitchen prep-time estimate** — how long until the order is ready
   for pickup. Modeled per-restaurant from historical prep times, order
   size, and current queue depth (a restaurant mid-rush takes longer per
   order than an empty one).
2. **Courier-to-restaurant time** — once a courier is matched, real-time
   routing (live traffic, road network) from the courier's current
   position to the restaurant.
3. **Restaurant-to-customer time** — routing from the restaurant to the
   delivery address, using the same live-traffic routing as step 2.

The customer-facing ETA is roughly `max(prep-done time, courier-arrival
time) + restaurant-to-customer time` — the courier can arrive before the
food is ready (then waits) or the food can be ready before the courier
arrives (then it waits), and the total only starts counting down the
final leg once both are done.

*Each stage has its own error bar and its own model — a bad prep-time
estimate at a busy restaurant shouldn't be blamed on the routing model,
and vice versa.*

ETAs are **recalculated periodically**, not computed once at order time,
because every input can change mid-order: a kitchen falls behind, a
courier hits traffic or takes a detour to fulfill a batched pickup, or a
courier declines an offer and re-matching adds a delay. Recomputing on a
short interval (and on key events — courier assigned, courier picked up)
keeps the countdown honest instead of silently drifting from reality
until the final "arriving now" jump.

<QuizItem
  question="Why is the customer-facing ETA modeled as max(prep-done time, courier-arrival time) + restaurant-to-customer time, rather than just prep-time + courier-travel-time + delivery-time added straight through?"
  answer="Because prep and courier travel happen in parallel, not in sequence — the courier can be en route while the kitchen is still cooking. The final delivery leg can only start once both are done, so the pipeline takes the max of the two parallel estimates before adding the sequential final leg."
/>

### Order lifecycle fan-out / notification

{/* concept-dependency: HLD-06 not yet built, explained inline */}
Ticketmaster's multi-party problem is a **saga** — one transactional flow
(hold → charge → confirm) coordinated by a single service, because a
seat can only be sold once and something has to own that decision.
Food Panda's multi-party problem has no such single-writer transaction:
customer, restaurant, and courier each mutate their *own* view of the
order independently, and nothing is being contended for. The system's
job is just making sure all three see every state change promptly, in
order, without polling a shared table.

- Every order-state transition (`placed`, `accepted`, `preparing`,
  `ready_for_pickup`, `courier_assigned`, `picked_up`, `delivered`,
  `cancelled`) is published as an event on the order-events topic shown
  in the architecture section.
- Each party's app holds a live connection (WebSocket/SSE) or push
  subscription keyed to their relevant orders; a fan-out consumer reads
  the event stream and pushes only the events each subscriber cares
  about — the restaurant doesn't need "courier location updated," the
  customer doesn't need "kitchen queue depth changed."
- **Ordering matters more than transactional atomicity here**: a
  customer seeing `picked_up` before `ready_for_pickup` looks broken even
  though no data was corrupted. Kafka's per-partition ordering (partition
  by `order_id`) guarantees each order's own events arrive at every
  consumer in the order they were published, without needing a
  distributed transaction across the three parties' views.

<QuizItem
  question="Food Panda's order lifecycle involves three independent parties, just like Ticketmaster's booking flow involves multiple steps. Why isn't Food Panda's lifecycle modeled as a saga the same way?"
  answer="A saga coordinates a single transactional flow with compensating actions because something is being contended for (a seat that can only sell once). In Food Panda, customer, restaurant, and courier each mutate only their own view of the order — nobody is racing for the same resource — so the actual problem is delivering every state change to all three promptly and in order, which per-order event ordering solves without needing saga-style compensation."
/>

### Restaurant availability & capacity signal

Matching a courier to an order that a restaurant can't actually make on
time wastes a courier's trip and a customer's wait. The system needs a
live **capacity signal** feeding into whether new orders are even
routed to a restaurant — the menu/inventory internals behind that signal
are out of scope, but the signal itself isn't.

- **Manual toggle** — the restaurant explicitly marks itself
  "busy"/"paused," simplest signal, depends on staff remembering to flip
  it.
- **Order-queue-depth auto-throttle** — the order service tracks how
  many orders are currently `accepted`/`preparing` for a restaurant
  against a configured capacity ceiling, and automatically stops routing
  new orders (or extends the quoted prep-time estimate) once the queue
  gets deep, without waiting on a human to notice.

Auto-throttle is the more reliable of the two because it reacts to
actual load rather than a staff member's attention during a dinner rush
— but it needs the manual toggle as an override for cases the queue
depth can't see (a broken oven, a walk-in line eating into kitchen
capacity).

The `ready_soon` signal fires 4 minutes before the kitchen's own
prep-done estimate — not at 0 minutes — precisely so Sam is already

## Interview angle

**"The offered courier declines, or the offer times out."** The matching
service re-offers to the next-ranked candidate from the same candidate
set immediately, rather than re-running the full geo-search from
scratch — the candidate pool from the original batch/search is still
valid for the next few seconds. If several offers in a row are declined,
that's itself a signal (low courier supply in the zone right now) worth
feeding back into ETA estimates rather than silently retrying forever.

**"The restaurant rejects the order after payment already went
through."** The order moves to a `rejected` state, and the order service
triggers a refund as an independent step against the payment provider —
the same pattern as Ticketmaster's compensating action on a failed
booking, but there's no seat to release here, just a payment to reverse
and the customer to notify. (Refund policy specifics stay out of scope,
per this lesson's problem statement — the interview-relevant shape is
that rejection and refund are two separate steps, not one.)

**"A courier goes offline mid-delivery."** Location ingestion detects a
gap once pings stop arriving past a threshold (e.g. 30-45 seconds with
no update). The customer-facing tracker falls back to the last-known
position plus a "having trouble reaching your courier" state rather than
silently freezing the map or showing a stale position as if it were
live — and support/dispatch gets an alert to check in or reassign if the
gap persists.

**"Scale the matching service across cities."** Matching is naturally
**geo-partitioned**: a courier in Berlin never competes for an order in
Manila, so the candidate search, batch-window solver, and offer/accept
logic can all run as independent per-city (or per-zone-within-a-city)
instances with no cross-partition coordination needed — the same
partitioning shape Ride-Sharing's matching service uses for the same
reason.

### Recap quiz

<QuizItem
  question="What makes Food Panda a three-sided marketplace rather than a two-sided one like Ride-Sharing?"
  answer="The restaurant is a third independent party whose prep time is a real production step the system can only estimate, not control — matching has to account for when the food will actually be ready, not just courier proximity, which a two-sided rider/driver match doesn't need to consider."
/>

<QuizItem
  question="Name two order-batching strategies and one situation where the matching service should avoid batching altogether."
  answer="Same-restaurant batching (two orders from one kitchen, one trip) and route-batching (orders from different nearby restaurants combined into one loop). Batching should be skipped for perishable/hot food with a tight quality window or an order with a promised ETA the added detour would blow through."
/>

<QuizItem
  question="List the three stages of the ETA pipeline and how they combine into the customer-facing countdown."
  answer="Kitchen prep-time estimate, courier-to-restaurant travel time, and restaurant-to-customer travel time. The countdown is roughly max(prep-done time, courier-arrival time) + restaurant-to-customer time, since prep and courier travel happen in parallel and only the final leg is sequential."
/>

<QuizItem
  question="Why does this system favor push (WebSocket/SSE) over polling for courier location updates, given the added connection-management cost?"
  answer="Push gets each location update to subscribed customers within about one round trip of the courier's phone sending it, with no wasted requests when the courier hasn't moved — polling's latency floor is the poll interval itself, and tracking staleness is a highly visible customer complaint this product can't tolerate."
/>

<QuizItem
  question="Why does Food Panda accept eventual consistency across the customer, restaurant, and courier views of one order, when Ticketmaster insists on strong consistency for seat state?"
  answer="Ticketmaster's seats are a scarce resource two buyers could both claim, so a stale read can lead to an unrecoverable double-sell. Food Panda's three parties each mutate only their own view of the order — nothing is being contended for — so a few seconds of staleness on one party's view has no correctness consequence, only a UX one."
/>

<QuizItem
  question="Why does the order-ready signal fire a few minutes before the kitchen's prep-done estimate, rather than exactly at it?"
  answer="Because matching and courier travel to the restaurant take time too. Firing early (e.g. 4 minutes before prep-done) means a courier is already en route and arrives close to when the food actually comes off the line, instead of the matching search only starting once the food is already sitting out cooling."
/>

<QuizItem
  question="Why is Food Panda's order lifecycle handled with event-driven fan-out rather than a saga like Ticketmaster's booking flow?"
  answer="A saga exists to coordinate compensating actions around a single contended resource. Food Panda's three parties never contend for the same resource — they each need to see the same sequence of state changes for an order they don't share a database row for, which per-order-partitioned event ordering solves without needing transactional coordination."

### Open design challenge

**Extend the matching service to support scheduled orders (deliver at a
specific future time) alongside on-demand orders.** Everything in this
lesson assumes an order wants a courier as soon as possible. Now a
customer can place an order at 2:00pm asking for delivery at 7:00pm.
Design how the matching service should change: when should the system
actually start the prep-time countdown and courier search for a
scheduled order — immediately, or closer to the requested time? How
should scheduled orders compete with on-demand orders for the same
courier pool during a busy window? What happens if the restaurant can't
actually confirm capacity for that future slot (already fully booked at
7:00pm)?

<details>
<summary>Reference approach (click to reveal)</summary>

- **When to start the countdown**: a scheduled order should sit in a
  `scheduled` state, invisible to the matching service, until a
  scheduler/cron-style trigger promotes it to the normal `placed` →
  prep-time-estimate flow at `requested_time − restaurant's typical prep
  time − buffer` — effectively re-entering the same pipeline this lesson
  already built, just with a delayed start rather than a new pipeline.
- **Competing for the courier pool**: don't give scheduled orders their
  own separate matching path — feeding them into the same
  `ready_soon` event at the right moment means they compete fairly
  through the existing batch-window solver, alongside genuinely on-demand
  orders arriving at the same moment. The one addition needed: the
  matching service should be able to see a short lookahead window of
  upcoming scheduled `ready_soon` events (not just what's ready *right
  now*) so it can hold a courier for an imminent scheduled order instead
  of sending them across town on an on-demand trip 2 minutes before the
  scheduled one needs them.
- **Restaurant capacity for a future slot**: extend the restaurant
  availability/capacity signal (covered in this lesson's deep dive) with
  a capacity check *at the requested future time*, not just current
  queue depth — a restaurant accepts or rejects the scheduled slot at
  order time, the same accept/reject step that already exists for
  on-demand orders, just evaluated against a projected future queue
  instead of the current one.
- **Failure mode to call out explicitly**: a scheduled order whose
  promotion trigger fires but finds the restaurant now closed or
  incapable (unexpected closure, no capacity) needs the same
  rejection-and-refund path this lesson already built for on-demand
  rejection — reusing the existing mechanism rather than building a
  second one.

</details>
